Ather battery warranty:
what each cover actually pays for

Ather now sells four levels of battery protection, from the standard three years to a ten-year add-on. Here is what the 70% promise really means, what a “free replacement” can legally be, and how to make a claim that gets approved.

Updated November 2026  ·  8-minute read  ·  For every Ather

Almost every question about Ather’s battery warranty comes down to one sentence: the battery is guaranteed to stay above 70% health for the length of the cover. That sentence is accurate, and it is also where most owners get it wrong — because the length of the cover depends on which plan you bought, and because “70% health” is not the same thing as “70% of your original range”.

This page pulls the structure apart using Ather’s own warranty policy and warranty page, so you can work out what you are actually entitled to before you book a service visit.

The short version: the warranty is a floor on capacity, not a promise about range. Standard cover is 3 years or 30,000 km. AtherStack Pro extends it to 5 years or 60,000 km, and two paid add-ons take it to 8 years or 80,000 km and 10 years or 1,00,000 km. If capacity drops below 70% inside the cover period, repair or replacement is free, labour included. Ather decides which of the two.

What the 70% promise actually says

The policy wording is specific, and it is worth knowing exactly: the battery’s nominal storage capacity must not degrade by more than 30% of the published nominal capacity during the warranty period. That is where the 70% figure comes from — it is a floor on measured pack capacity, tested by Ather’s own procedure, not a promise that your scooter will still do its brochure range.

Why the difference matters:

  • Capacity and range are related but not identical. Losing 30% of capacity costs you roughly 30% of the energy you can store. What that does to kilometres depends on riding mode, load, tyre pressure, temperature and traffic.
  • A warranty claim is assessed on a reading, not on a feeling. “My range feels low in winter” is not a measurement. A verified State of Health number below the threshold is.
  • Early readings are not evidence. Ather’s own FAQ says battery health data becomes reliable only after about 10,000 km, because a new pack’s numbers move while it settles.

So the useful question before any claim is not “does my range feel shorter” but “what does the pack actually read, and is that below 70% while I am still inside the cover?” That is a measurement you can take yourself, and we come back to it below.

Two details from the policy that most owners never hear:

  • The 450 Apex battery has a longer standard period. The policy lists the Apex battery at 5 years or 60,000 km even at the standard level, while other models start at 3 years or 30,000 km. If you ride an Apex, read your paperwork before assuming the shorter term applies.
  • Whichever limit comes first applies. A high-mileage rider can exit the kilometre cap years before the calendar cap, and the cover ends there. 80,000 km at 15,000 km a year is about five and a half years, not eight.

What “free replacement” means in practice

The word “replacement” in marketing material and the word “repair” in the policy are the same obligation. Ather’s stated position is that it will repair or replace at its discretion, and the policy allows it to use new, refurbished or reconditioned genuine parts that have passed its functional tests. Three consequences worth understanding:

  • You may get a repaired pack, not a new one. That is within the terms, and in practice a pack with one weak cell group is often repaired rather than replaced.
  • The replacement must match what you had, not what you bought new. The policy says the energy capacity of the replacement will be at least equal to the original battery immediately before the failure, taking age and mileage into account. A four-year-old pack therefore does not have to come back at 100%.
  • The warranty on the replaced part runs only for the remainder of the original term. A pack replaced in year four of an eight-year cover is covered for the remaining four years, not for a fresh eight.

Labour is included when the work is done under warranty, and Ather’s Eight70 FAQ states there is no upper limit on the claim amount. Transport costs to get the scooter to a service centre are not part of the cover.

What actually voids a battery warranty

The exclusions list is standard for the industry, with one genuinely unusual inclusion:

  • Modifications or alterations to the battery, including opening the pack or fitting non-genuine cells.
  • Tampering with the VIN or BIN (vehicle and battery identification numbers).
  • Accident, impact, theft, flood, fire, or physical damage to the pack or its casing.
  • Negligence — but with an explicit carve-out: deep discharge from a scooter sitting idle and uncharged is covered. That is more generous than most two-wheeler warranties, and it matters if your scooter spends months parked.
  • Not reporting a defect promptly. The policy expects you to raise a problem within days of noticing it, not at the end of the cover period.

What is not on that list: reading data from the diagnostic port. More on that below.

How to make a claim that gets approved

The claim itself is a documentation exercise, and this is where owners lose weeks. Work in this order:

  1. Record a dated baseline before anything else. Note the odometer, the State of Health, the cell delta and the date. Two readings a few weeks apart show a trend; one reading can always be dismissed as an estimate.
  2. Describe the problem in numbers. “SOH reads 69% at 41,200 km and my cell delta has climbed from 40 to 90 mV in six months” is a claim. “Range feels low” is a conversation.
  3. Book the service visit and say what you want measured. Ask for the battery health reading and, if the pack is opened, the cell-level report.
  4. Get their reading in writing when you hand the scooter over, so you can compare it with yours afterwards.
  5. Keep everything — screenshots, job cards, emails. They also happen to be exactly what a buyer asks for when you sell.

If your scooter is outside the cover period, none of this is wasted: a verified reading is what tells you whether you are looking at a ₹10,000–₹15,000 repair or a full pack, which is the subject of the replacement cost guide.

Does the warranty transfer if you sell?

Yes. The cover follows the vehicle, not the owner, and it starts on the invoice date rather than the delivery date or the date a warranty add-on was claimed. A used Ather with three years of Eight70 left on it is meaningfully more valuable than one without — which is why the paperwork you keep in step 5 is worth money later.

Where a diagnostic reader fits in

The reader A-OBD sells is passive. It plugs into the diagnostic connector in the boot and listens to the CAN bus; it never writes to the scooter, never changes firmware and never splices a wire. That is the same thing a workshop laptop does when a service centre reads your pack, so there is nothing in it for a warranty to object to.

The honest caveat, which applies to any accessory: no gadget can control how an individual service centre treats your scooter on the day. If you are mid-claim, mention it rather than hiding it, and bring your own readings as backup rather than as an argument.
